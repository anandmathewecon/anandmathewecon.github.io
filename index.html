Research

For households living on daily farm or construction wages, a drought or a month of heavy rain means lost pay, and the family still has to eat. My job market paper, with Sankalp Mathur of CAFRAL, asks how these families get through, and what happens when the one formal lender they relied on disappears. We follow more than 200,000 Indian households through weather shocks and the 2018 collapse in non-bank lending, matching household survey data to credit bureau records. Where credit was still available, families cut entertainment, meals out and new clothes, and kept spending on health care, schooling and staples like milk. Where the lender had withdrawn, the same shock pushed them into cutting those too. Fewer than half of the families who lost their lender found another source of credit. The rest stopped borrowing altogether.

I also work on drug policy and education in the United States, using Washington State's lottery for cannabis retail licenses to estimate how a nearby dispensary affects whether college students return for a second year. Papers and abstracts
