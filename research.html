Job Market Paper

Credit Where Credit Is Withdrawn: Weather Shocks and the Household Coping Portfolio in India

with Sankalp Mathur (CAFRAL)

Weather shocks cut income in India, and household consumption barely moves. The null has been found often enough to look like a fact about insurance. We argue it is a fact about the outcome variable. Total spending does not respond because households change what they buy rather than how much, and a regression on the level looks for a change in scale that never occurs. In a panel of 210,942 Indian households observed from 2014 to 2019 and matched to 1.08 billion loan records from the credit bureau, spending cuts begin with the postponable, from entertainment and restaurant meals down through clothing, and stop before cereals, which do not fall at all. The September 2018 default of Infrastructure Leasing and Financial Services, a funding shock that bound hardest in districts reliant on non-bank lenders, then lets us ask what formal credit contributes. Where the lender withdrew, the same weather shock pushes households further along the ordering, into health, education, and staples such as milk, none of which move where credit held. Displaced borrowers mostly do not resurface at moneylenders. More stop borrowing altogether than switch, so losing formal access looks closer to rationing than redirection. Availability of credit does not stop a weather shock from being felt. It determines how far down the coping portfolio a household has to go.

Working Papers

Cannabis Access and College Persistence: Evidence from Washington State's Dispensary Lottery

Single-authored

Abstract

This paper examines how proximity to recreational marijuana dispensaries affects postsecondary educational outcomes in Washington State. Leveraging the quasi-random placement of dispensaries via a state-run license lottery and a staggered difference-in-differences framework, I estimate the causal impact of dispensary access on college enrollment and retention rates. Using campus-level administrative data from the Integrated Postsecondary Education Data System (IPEDS) and detailed dispensary location data, I find that institutions located within a 10-minute driving radius of a dispensary experienced a statistically significant 7.9 percentage point decline in student retention rates. In contrast, there is no evidence that dispensary proximity affects college enrollment. Robustness checks across alternative distance thresholds confirm that the retention effect is stable and significant for thresholds between 10 and 15 minutes, while enrollment results remain null. These findings suggest that while local cannabis availability may not deter students from enrolling, it can negatively affect persistence among those already enrolled.
